1. Leadership is a practice

Leadership is not authority alone; it is the practice of mobilising people to address adaptive challenges and build human capability.

  • Not defined by title or authority.
  • Can occur with or without formal authority.
  • The practice of mobilising people.
  • Builds capacity in people and organisations.
  • Helps groups respond to adaptive challenges.
  • Deliberate behavioural practice.
Lead without authority
→

→
Respond to adaptive challenges
→

Practising leadership does not require authority.

2. Leadership is not the same as authority

Authority can give power, but leadership is framed as service, integrity and confidence to solve problems together.

Authority

Power may be entrusted from A to B.

Leadership

Service flows back into the relationship; people are mobilised to solve the problem together.

Medicine analogy: experts can do a lot, but if patients do not participate in the solution, the problem will not be solved.

“The solution to a problem needs to be owned by the owner of the problem.”

Leadership exists at all levels and is best viewed as a practice.

3. Technical vs adaptive challenges

Technical and adaptive work differ in solution certainty, participation and learning requirements.

Technical challenges

  • Known solutions exist.
  • Can be solved with expertise & authority.
  • Focus on getting the problem “right”.
  • Relatively predictable.

Adaptive challenges

  • No known solution.
  • Require learning & behaviour change.
  • Need participation & ownership.
  • Unpredictable & complex.
Continuum of challenges: Technical → Mixed → Adaptive / Wicked. Challenges are often blended, and leadership is required when answers are unclear.

4. Time frames of technical & adaptive work

Adaptive work is improvisational: leaders regulate disequilibrium so people stay inside a productive range of stress.

Stay in the productive range of stress
  • Adaptive change is higher-stress and involves uncertainty.
  • Too little stress = no learning.
  • Too much stress = disengagement and work avoidance.
  • Leaders regulate stress to keep teams in the learning zone.
  • Ownership and participation are essential.
Threshold of learningEnough disequilibrium to create learning.
Productive range of stressAdaptive learning stays active without overwhelming the group.
Limit of toleranceBeyond this, people shift toward work avoidance.
Protect teams from going above tolerance while still remaining inside the productive learning zone.

  • Listen before advocating.
Leadership is about relationships and participation, not just positional power.

7. Vertical development & feedback maturity

Feedback maturity develops from defensive responses toward curiosity, blind-spot discovery and partnership.

  1. 1 — Feedback as attack: defend, blame, shut down.
  2. 2 — Feedback as disapproval: defensive, rationalise, externalise.
  3. 3 — Feedback to defend position: justify, explain, protect own view.
  4. 4 — Feedback to improve goals: open to input to achieve better outcomes.
  5. 5 — Feedback to uncover hidden behaviour: open, self-aware, seeks blind spots.
  6. 6 — Feedback as partnership & shared development: curious, growth-focused, co-created.
Feedback→Reflection→Behaviour change→Feedback growth loop
More developed leaders become increasingly curious about feedback.

Stress can become a resource when regulated deliberately.

Test small, learn fast, scale faster

A conversation-led model of interviewing: structure, curiosity, listening and connection.

An interview is structured — but it should feel human

The interviewer owns the structure and keeps the focus on the other person. The craft is to make a high-stakes interaction feel like a real conversation, not an interrogation.

Five techniques for better questions

The emphasis is not on ‘clever’ questions. It is on preparation, curiosity, listening, playfulness and knowing when to stop pushing.

Trust + connection = influence

The interviewing loop is relational: listen to understand, connect to build rapport, respond to add value, and use feedback to strengthen the loop.

  3. Find some common ground as just as possible.
  4. Be transparent and professional.

  1. Build real empathy — genuinely listen, “give a sh*t”.

6. Five techniques to ask better questions

Preparation matters, but strong interviewers stay curious, listen and adapt rather than interrogate.

Curiosity beats interrogation
  1. Plan vs in the moment: courage to follow the plan, plan to go back.
  2. Curiosity vs interrogation: careful of the weight of the question.
  3. Shut up & listen.
  4. Be playful: people deal with life with humour.
  5. Don’t be afraid to move on: don’t keep pushing to get an answer.

7. Close the conversation positively

The ending should preserve trust, acknowledge effort and leave the person clear on what happens next.

  1. Plan how you bring them out — key learning question.
  2. Acknowledge the effort.
  3. Clear about next steps — step with a bow.
  4. Be consistent.

2. Functional vs systemic understanding

A systemic view requires more than knowing how the parts work; it requires seeing how the work actually happens across the whole.

Functional understanding

Know how the parts work. Useful for bounded work; weaker for complex whole-system change.

Systemic understanding

Know how the whole system works together and how groups interact in the real work.

Observation 1: “Upstream work often requires us to re-integrate groups that we separated intentionally.”
You need to go and see the work to understand the system. Do not rely on surveys, benchmarks or data alone.

5. AI Impact Framework — opportunities & threats from AI

The slide frames AI impact across three dimensions, increasing in magnitude toward systemic disruption.

A. Productivity
  1. Task automation: repetitive, low-value tasks; removes inefficiency in existing workflows.
  2. Workflow redesign: embeds AI across entire workflows for system-level performance.
  3. AI-native production systems: operations largely autonomous with human oversight.
B. Proposition innovation
  1. Improved customer experience: tailoring products / pricing using AI within existing structures.
  2. Product & service innovation: new or enhanced AI-enabled products and services.
  3. Business model reinvention: new customer value through changed delivery / pricing models.
C. Transaction cost reduction
  1. Improved discoverability: easier search and comparison.
  2. Increased switching: reduced switching friction and automated switching.
  3. Disintermediation: shifts who owns the customer through agentic interfaces.
Systemic disruption: reshaping industry structure, competitive landscape, customer relationships and value pools.

6. AI moats — 9 sources of competitive advantage

The slide identifies nine sources of advantage and describes how each can remain defensible in an AI world.

1. Economies of scaleShift costs toward fixed platform investment and turn scale into compounding unit-cost advantage.
2. Privileged dataUse proprietary data to improve model performance and build data flywheels competitors cannot replicate.
3. EmbeddednessDeep integration into systems, workflows and transactions creates structural dependence and stickiness.
4. Network effectsScale improves matching, engagement and outcomes, increasing value with interaction.
5. Constrained assetsCombine intelligence with physical, operational or regulated assets that require capital / scale to replicate.
6. Business model disruptionShift customer relationships or revenue models using interlocking choices that are difficult to copy.
7. Rewiring for velocityIncrease speed of experimentation, learning and deployment to compound execution advantage.
8. Regulation & complianceEmbed governance, auditability and risk controls into core systems for scalable, defensible advantage.
9. TrustAnchor relationships in safety, transparency and reliability to unlock adoption, data access and long-term advantage.
